Paul Skenes 2026: full picture breakdown

Offseason bullpens, spring training, the World Baseball Classic and the regular season, on one set of conventions. What held from the winter, what changed, and what the changes did and did not cause.

Three things changed between the winter and the summer, and they are not the same thing. His rubber setup went from a rehearsed two-position system to a single spot. His velocity and extension built cleanly and then held all season. His fastball spin fell and kept falling. The first is a mechanical decision, the second is a healthy build, and the third is unexplained by either.

The three storylines

Release position — a system built, then abandoned

Fifteen of seventeen bullpens show alternating work between two rubber positions 7.0 inches apart. Those positions map onto right- and left-handed hitters in spring and the Classic to within half an inch. By July the distinction is gone.

Velocity and extension — the build worked

Both climb together from December into spring and then stay put. Nothing in this channel looks like a problem at any point in the year.

Spin — falling, and not because of release

Fastball spin runs from 2,405 rpm in March to about 2,120 by July and does not recover when the release does. Whatever is driving it is not in the release geometry.

Every bullpen from December 12 through February 6 splits into alternating runs of pitches at two distinct release positions. Not scatter — blocks. He throws a set from one spot, moves, throws a set from the other.

The pattern appears in 15 of 17 sessions. The two exceptions are the seven-pitch and ten-pitch bullpens, too small to detect blocks in — a limit of the sample, not evidence he skipped it.

They match the game exactly

Through spring, the Classic and opening day he released 6.9 inches further to the arm side against left-handed hitters. Set that beside the bullpen marks.

Then it came apart

April inverts the sign. May and June shrink it. By July the split is three tenths of an inch, which is nothing — he is throwing from one spot to everybody.

This is the question worth your movement data. A system drilled across three months and executed cleanly in March does not usually vanish in eight weeks by accident. April is the window: the inversion happens between opening day and the second start.

This is the healthy part of the year. Velocity and extension rise together through the offseason, arrive at game levels by spring, and hold from there.

The dip through late December and early January is the holiday lull — extension bottoms at 4.65 ft on December 31 and velocity with it. From January 15 the build is continuous.

The gap from the last bullpen to the first spring outing is 6.4 mph and 0.44 ft of extension.

That is the bullpen-to-game effort offset, measured rather than assumed. It is the number to apply whenever a bullpen state is compared to a game state, and it is why raw bullpen extension should never be read against a game figure directly.

What holds all season

In-season fastball extension sits between 6.73 and 6.90 ft every month from March to August, and velocity between 96.5 and 97.9 mph. Neither channel drifts. Whatever changed this year, the drive down the mound did not.

Fastball spin fell from 2,405 rpm in March to roughly 2,120 by July. Induced vertical break followed it down. This is the one channel in the year that looks like a genuine loss, and the release point does not explain it.

Testing the release hypothesis

The original question was whether release position drives the loss. Both drift over the same months and the raw correlation between distance-from-mark and spin is −0.30, which looks promising until calendar time enters the model.

With time controlled, distance from the nearest rehearsed mark returns t = +0.41 against spin, t = −0.43 against velocity and t = −1.28 against induced vertical break. The same test across sinker, sweeper, splinker and changeup returns nothing significant. Substituting release consistency for release position gets closest, at t = −1.84 for fastball release scatter against spin, which is suggestive and no more.

In August he returns to within a third of an inch of his bullpen mark — the closest all season — and spin stays flat at July levels.

That is the test that settles it. If position drove the loss, returning to position should recover it. It does not. Two things declining over the same summer, not one causing the other.

This also corrects an earlier read. The late-August release movement previously flagged as a shift toward the glove side is neither glove-side nor a departure. It is a move back to the arm side, onto the offseason mark. July was the anomaly.

Still open

Fatigue, grip, ball or seam variation, and conditions are all untested here. None of them are ruled out; none of them are supported either. The one thing this analysis can say is that the answer is not release geometry.

Conventions

Release side and height are reported at the true release point for both bullpen and game data, with the bullpen sign inverted to match. More negative is further to the arm side. Game figures were re-planed from the fixed tracking plane before joining, a correction worth 0.21 ft horizontally — the same order as the shifts under study. Bullpen pitch types were classified by shape rather than by the portal's tags, which were absent or wrong on roughly half the sessions.
